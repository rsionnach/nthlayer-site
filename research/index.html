The AI reliability gap almost nobody is measuring

Every observability tool we reviewed monitored the mechanics of AI: latency, tokens, cost. As of March 2026, none of them measured whether AI agents were making good decisions. Practitioners and analysts were describing the same gap from different directions.

The numbers

  • 89% of organisations surveyed had some form of observability for their agents LangChain, Dec 2025 [1]
  • #1 barrier to production was still quality LangChain, Dec 2025 [1]
  • 69% of agentic AI decisions were verified by a human Dynatrace, Jan 2026 [2]
  • 40%+ of agentic AI projects forecast to be cancelled by the end of 2027 Gartner forecast, Jun 2025 [3]

The thesis

By March 2026 the observability industry had converged on a single strategy for AI: making AI better at investigating incidents. Every vendor we reviewed had an MCP integration. Every vendor had a chatbot. Every vendor had a demo where the AI found root cause in thirty seconds.

Almost nobody was asking the other question: are the AI agents themselves making good decisions?

This research examined 9 established observability vendors, 14 AI-specific startups, 6 governance platforms, the major open-source standards, analyst reports from Gartner and Forrester, and conference talks from SREcon, QCon and KubeCon. The finding was consistent: every tool we reviewed monitored the mechanics of AI (latency, tokens, cost, error rates), while decision quality (reversal rates, confidence calibration, high-confidence failure rates) remained unmeasured in production.

The gap was not subtle, and people building and operating these systems were saying so in public.

What the vendors offered

A cross-vendor analysis showed near-total convergence. Every vendor we reviewed tracked the same AI metrics: latency, token usage, costs, error rates, and prompt/response traces. Several added hallucination detection and toxicity scanning. None of those we reviewed measured decision quality as an SLO with error budgets.

Two conflations

“AI for Ops” vs. “Ops for AI”

Thoughtworks articulated a distinction that, in our review, the market was consistently failing to make: AI for Ops is about optimising IT operations; Ops for AI is about making AI systems trustworthy, safe, reliable and cost-effective. Collapsing the two into a single definition, they write, “erases the nuance — and risks applying the wrong playbook to the wrong problem.” [9]

Every established vendor we reviewed was primarily investing in AI for Ops: using AI to help humans investigate incidents faster. Almost nobody was building Ops for AI: measuring whether AI systems themselves are reliable.

Governance vs. reliability

The AI agent governance platforms we reviewed (Microsoft Agent Governance Toolkit, CortexHub, AControlLayer, Kore.ai, Acuvity, Aegis) focused exclusively on pre-execution authorisation: is this agent allowed to do this? None of them systematically measured post-execution decision quality: did this agent make a good decision?

JetBrains put it clearly: “Governance frameworks define permissions, approval gates, and high-level constraints. They rarely account for how an agent interprets context, chains tools, or explores edge cases while attempting to complete a task. The result is a structural gap.” [10]

The gap between governance and reliability is the space between the bouncer checking your ID at the door and the auditor reviewing whether the decisions made inside were sound. The industry had the bouncer. It didn’t have the auditor.

No open standard we found supported judgment SLOs

At the time of review, OpenTelemetry had merged evaluation event conventions (v1.38.0, October 2025) with attributes for generic evaluation scores [13]. The agentic systems proposal (Issue #2664) covered tasks, actions, agents, teams, artifacts and memory, but no decision quality semantics [14]: no gen_ai.decision.* or gen_ai.override.* attributes.

OpenSLO v1.0 supported traditional ratio-based SLIs. It had no concept of judgment quality, observation periods, confidence-weighted metrics or human override signals [15].

All three major SLO generators (Sloth, Pyrra, Google SLO Generator) produced Prometheus rules exclusively for availability and latency SLOs. None supported AI decision quality metrics [16].

OpenSRM (the open specification NthLayer is built on) was the only open specification we found, as of March 2026, that models AI decision quality as an SLO, with constructs for reversal rate, high-confidence failure rate above a confidence_threshold, calibration as a threshold, and measurement windows [17].

Compound failure and the Jevons paradox

The Jevons paradox is the observation that when something becomes cheaper to use, people use more of it, so total consumption rises instead of falling. Applied to software: when code is cheaper to write, we write more of it, and complexity goes up. By early 2026 that argument had data behind it.

  • 56% year-over-year increase in reported AI-related incidents (2024 vs 2023) Stanford AI Index 2025 [11]
  • 16% year-over-year increase in incidents at enterprise companies PagerDuty, Feb 2024 [12]
  • ~20% success rate for a 10-step AI agent at 85% per-step accuracy 0.8510 ≈ 0.197 — compound probability, illustrative

The compound failure math is unforgiving. An AI agent with 85% per-step accuracy succeeds only about 20% of the time on a 10-step task. As organisations run dozens or hundreds of agents that interact with each other and with traditional services, individual agent monitoring cannot detect compound failures that emerge from interactions between agents.

The structural gap

The research found a market that was simultaneously booming and structurally incomplete. Billions were flowing into observability tooling, and every major vendor had shipped an AI monitoring product. Yet the fundamental question, is this AI agent making good decisions?, was answered by no shipping product we reviewed.

Three findings stood out:

  1. An engineer working on AI reliability at Anthropic said in a public talk that Claude repeatedly misread a lost cache as a capacity problem, and that a correction covers only that case: it “will get wrong correlation versus causation in the other 99 situations.” We found no shipping product that measured how often that kind of judgment turns out wrong.
  2. The LangChain survey, with 89% reporting observability while quality remained the #1 barrier, suggests the gap is not an observability gap in the traditional sense. It is a decision quality measurement gap.
  3. Discussing security attacks on AI agents, Gartner stated that “guardrails, security filters, human oversight, or even security observability are not sufficient to ensure consistently appropriate agent use.” In our reading, closing that gap takes something other than more of the same controls.

The gaps followed from architectural choices. Established vendors built around metrics, logs and traces for infrastructure. Startups built around SDK instrumentation for individual model evaluation. Governance platforms built around pre-execution policy gates.

Where NthLayer fits

NthLayer is our answer to the gap this research describes: measuring whether decisions hold up, in the monitoring you already run.

  • Decisions no test can check are recorded as verdicts. Each verdict is written once, when the decision is made. Its outcome settles once, later: confirmed when a positive outcome is posted, overridden when a human overrides it, expired after 7 days with no signal. The human verification in Dynatrace’s numbers is exactly this kind of signal.
  • Judgment SLOs compile into the Prometheus and Grafana you run. Targets, measurement windows and breach actions are declared in an OpenSRM manifest. Today the reversal rate is overrides ÷ decisions, computed from Prometheus counters.
  • The flagship case: measuring a third-party AI SRE. Every triage, suppression or remediation it makes becomes a verdict, the on-call’s overrides settle them, and its reversal rate is held to a target.
  • NthLayer executes nothing. Its respond worker only proposes; a human approves and acts. When a judgment SLO breaches, reduce_autonomy is the breach action declared in the manifest, and your platform dispatches it.
  • External to the agent. NthLayer doesn’t instrument the agent’s internals or ask the agent to grade itself. The manifest declares what reliable means; the measurement comes from outside the agent.
